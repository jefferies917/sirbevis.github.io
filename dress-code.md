# Dress Codes

## Our Dress Code

Sir Bevis Lodge’s usual dress code on their Summons is Dress: Dark, Dinner Suit or
Morning Suit, Craft, Black or Provincial Tie, White Collar &amp; White Gloves. Most Lodges in the
area have the same code.

## Masonic Dress Codes - A Short History

Most people know that Masonic Lodges have dress codes. The First World War contributed
to these in at least two ways. First Freemasons in the armed forces moving between
postings, sometimes to the front in France, did not always have space within their kit for the
civilian clothing expected in Lodges. Lodges with armed services connections had always
allowed uniforms within the Lodge. Other Lodges picked this up and started to allow armed
service members to attend a Lodge in their uniforms. After the War Lodges just continued to
allow the wearing of uniform by members of the armed services. It is a little rarity these days,
but Lodges, with no connection to armed services, operating near military camps, naval
bases and air force bases still allow uniform within Lodge. The writer has attended a Lodge
in Fareham, near Portsmouth, where two naval personnel were in uniform, and a Lodge in
Winchester with an Army Medic in uniform.

Talking of uniforms reminds one of the Master Mason of one Hampshire Lodge who whilst
serving in the Royal Navy was assigned to the Royal Yacht Brittania. This meant he went
wherever Her Majesty the Queen went in the World. He used to say that their were five
Freemasons serving on the Yacht, and when they knew how long they would be in a foreign
port and who was not on duty those nights, they would contact United Grand Lodge of
England in London and ask if a Lodge was going to be open in the port and permission to
attend. UGLE would reply as needed and if a meeting was being held the group would then
turn up to the Lodge in their “dress” uniforms, prove themselves Freemasons, and attend the
Lodge. He said they warmly greeted except on two occasions. One was in South America
where the Lodge did not have any English speakers who could check their English Masonic
credentials and they, the sailors, did not have sufficient Spanish to do it in that language.
The other was in California, where the Lodge refused them entry as they were not in
tuxedoes!

The second effect of the First World War was that when it ended the UK Masonic Bodies
said that Freemasonry should be in mourning for those who had died in the war. They
ordered that mourning dress be worn in Lodges. This meant black suit; black tie, socks and
shoes; white shirt was compulsory. And that standard of dress continued for some time.

It has been indicated that Grand Lodge were considering scrapping the mourning dress
order when the Second World War started; so, it was put on the back burner. It remained
there until the late 1980’s when Grand Lodge decided to cancel it and to allow Lodges to
have their own dress codes. Nothing happened at first; after all the members were used to
wear that form of dress for Lodge, Some Lodges with associations with schools adopted the
old school tie for their brethren. Some of them will also accept brethren from other Lodges to
attend and wear their own old school tie. The various Provinces within England also created
ties for wearing to Lodges within their jurisdiction. It is now common to see a range of ties
within a Lodges, although the dark suit etc still prevails.

As stated, ties are now a distinguishing matter within Lodges, but on one occasion at
Southampton University Lodge there was a visitor from an Oxford University Lodge who was
wearing blue socks with the otherwise normal dress. Asked about this he said his Lodge
wore socks in Oxford University blue!

Mention has been made of brethren not being dressed within the code turning up at a
Lodge. This happened at Woolston based Lodge many years back. The Lodge did not have
a normal ceremony arranged so they had asked the Provincial Lecture team to give a lecture
during the Lodge. The Lecture Team mentioned online they were to lecture in Woolston with
the date and time. This meant a number of visiting brethren attended, all but one were

dressed in the usual dark suit etc. The one who did not wear his suit had thought it was just
a lecture, without a Lodge meeting occurring: so, he was casually dressed. Rather than turn
him away a quick survey of the other brethren found that one brother always carried a spare
white shirt which he lent the “casual” brother, another produced his spare tie as a loan. The
“casual” brother was admitted, sat in the back row with “his” white shirt and black tie visible
to all. In the spirit of Freemasonry, he had not been tuned away.

Sir Bevis Lodge’s usual dress code on their Summons is Dress: Dark, Dinner Suit or
Morning Suit, Craft, Black or Provincial Tie, White Collar &amp; White Gloves. Most Lodges in the
area have the same code.

If your Lodge’s dress code is not like this, please let us know, and how strictly is it enforced?
