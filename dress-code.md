# Dress Codes

## Our Dress Code

**Sir Bevis Lodge’s usual dress code** on their Summons is:  
*Dress: Dark, Dinner Suit or Morning Suit, Craft, Black or Provincial Tie, White Collar & White Gloves.*

Most Lodges in the area have the **same code**.

## Masonic Dress Codes – *A Short History*

Most people know that **Masonic Lodges have dress codes**. The *First World War* contributed to these in at least two ways.

First, **Freemasons in the armed forces**, moving between postings — sometimes to the front in *France* — did not always have space within their kit for the **civilian clothing** expected in Lodges.  

Lodges with **armed services connections** had always allowed *uniforms* within the Lodge. Other Lodges picked this up and started to allow **armed service members** to attend in uniform.

After the War, Lodges simply **continued this practice**.

It is a *little rarity these days*, but Lodges with no direct connection to the armed services — yet located near **military camps, naval bases, or air force bases** — may still allow uniforms.

The writer has attended:
- A Lodge in **Fareham (near Portsmouth)** with *naval personnel in uniform*  
- A Lodge in **Winchester** with an *Army Medic in uniform*

---

Talking of uniforms reminds one of a **Master Mason** of a Hampshire Lodge who, while serving in the **Royal Navy**, was assigned to the *Royal Yacht Britannia*.  

This meant he travelled wherever **Her Majesty the Queen** went.

He explained that there were *five Freemasons* serving on the Yacht. When they knew how long they would be in a **foreign port**, and who was off duty, they would:

1. Contact the **United Grand Lodge of England (UGLE)** in London  
2. Ask whether a Lodge meeting would be held locally  
3. Request **permission to attend**

If approved, they would attend in their **“dress” uniforms**, prove themselves Freemasons, and join the meeting.

They were *warmly welcomed* — except on **two occasions**:

- In **South America**, where language barriers prevented verification  
- In **California**, where they were refused entry for *not wearing tuxedos*

---

The **second effect** of the *First World War* was more symbolic.

After the war, UK Masonic bodies declared that **Freemasonry should be in mourning** for those who had died. Lodges were instructed to wear **mourning dress**, consisting of:

- **Black suit**
- **Black tie, socks, and shoes**
- **White shirt (compulsory)**

This standard continued for *many years*.

---

It was later suggested that **Grand Lodge** might remove the mourning requirement — but the *Second World War* delayed this decision.

It remained unchanged until the **late 1980s**, when Grand Lodge finally:
- **Cancelled the mourning dress requirement**
- Allowed Lodges to adopt their own **dress codes**

At first, little changed — members were *used to the tradition*.

---

Over time, however, variations appeared:

- Some Lodges adopted **old school ties**
- Visitors were sometimes allowed to wear *their own school ties*
- Provinces introduced their own **distinctive ties**

Today, it is common to see a **variety of ties** in Lodge, although the **dark suit standard still prevails**.

---

One notable example occurred at **Southampton University Lodge**, where a visitor from an **Oxford University Lodge** wore *blue socks* with otherwise standard attire.

When asked, he explained:  
*“In our Lodge, we wear Oxford blue socks.”*

---

There have also been occasions where brethren arrived *not properly dressed*.

At a **Woolston-based Lodge**, during a meeting featuring a **Provincial Lecture Team**, a visiting brother arrived in *casual clothing*, mistakenly believing it was *only a lecture*.

Rather than turn him away:

- One brother lent a **spare white shirt**
- Another provided a **spare tie**

The visitor was admitted and sat at the back, with his *borrowed attire visible to all*.

**In the true spirit of Freemasonry, he was not turned away.**

---

## Summary

**Sir Bevis Lodge’s dress code remains:**

*Dark suit, dinner suit or morning suit, Craft, black or Provincial tie, white collar & white gloves.*

Most Lodges in the area follow a **similar standard**.

---

If your Lodge’s dress code is *different*, please let us know — and **how strictly is it enforced?**
